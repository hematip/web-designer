You have noticed something: a repeated question, a missed appointment, a name that used to come easily. You want to say something, and you are afraid of how it will land. Most adult children who have had this conversation say the same thing afterwards: it went more easily than they had feared, and they wished they had started sooner.

## Before you say anything

Notice for a while. Keep a short private note of what you see and when: not to build a case, but so that you can describe specific things rather than a vague worry. Specific is kinder. "You asked me twice about Sunday" is something your mother can think about; "your memory is going" is something she can only deny.

Think about what you want from the conversation. Usually it is one thing: an agreement to see the doctor together.

## Choosing the moment

Pick a calm time with no audience: a walk, a drive, a cup of tea after lunch. Not at a family gathering, not after an incident, not when either of you is tired. One conversation will probably not be enough, and that is fine.

## Words that help

- Start with yourself. "I've noticed a couple of things and they're on my mind."
- Ask before telling. "Have you noticed anything with your memory lately?" Many parents have, and are relieved to be asked.
- Keep it ordinary. A memory check is a check-up, like blood pressure. Many things that affect memory are simple to find and to put right: sleep, thyroid, a medicine that doesn't suit.
- Offer to go together. "I'll come with you, and we'll ask about it together."
- Leave the door open. If the answer is no, say you will ask again, and do.

## What to avoid

Avoid the word dementia in the first conversation, unless your parent uses it first. Avoid testing them in the moment ("what did we do yesterday?"). Avoid correcting every small mistake in front of others; it humiliates and teaches them to hide things from you. And avoid making the decision for them. Independence is worth protecting, and a parent who feels in charge of the process is far more likely to go through with it.

## Turning it into a visit

Book the appointment while you are both still agreed. Before it, write down together what you have both noticed, and gather every medicine and supplement in one bag. At the visit, let your parent speak first. The doctor will want their account as well as yours.

If this becomes a longer road, your own sleep, health and company matter: for you, and for the care you give. There is more for caregivers on this site, and the page on who Memowell® is for has a note written for you.
